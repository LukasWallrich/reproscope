# Automated statistical review and multiverse robustness testing: tools and literature

Scan date: 2026-08-31. Every DOI and arXiv ID below was resolved against the Crossref API, the arXiv API, or the publisher landing page. Items that could not be confirmed are marked **[unverified]** — treat those as leads, not citations.

**The three numbers that frame the whole design space:**

| Task | Best published performance | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Recompute a reported p-value from its test statistic (deterministic) | sensitivity 85.3–100%, specificity 96.0–100% — **but only on the ~61% of tests it parses** | statcheck validity study + Schmidt critique |
| Find a *seeded* claim-invalidating error in a real paper, 10 guesses allowed | **39.1%** (GPT-5) | FLAWS, arXiv:2511.21843 |
| Find a *real* error that caused an erratum or retraction | **21.1% recall at 6.1% precision** (o3) | SPOT, arXiv:2505.11855 |

Precision is bought by narrowing scope, not by better prompting. The one high-precision LLM result (83.2%) came from restricting the model to arithmetic, formulas, figures and tables and excluding interpretive judgement entirely.

## 1. Automated statistics checking (numerical consistency)

### 1.1 statcheck

**What it does.** Four steps: convert PDF/HTML to plain text; regex-match null-hypothesis tests reported in complete APA style (t, F, r, z, χ², Q); recompute p from the reported test statistic and df; compare reported vs recomputed. Two error classes: `error` (reported p ≠ recomputed p) and `decision_error` (the two fall on opposite sides of α). Rounding of the test statistic is handled; one-tailed testing is an option, not a default.

**Hard scope limit.** The result must carry test statistic + df + p-value in APA order. Results missing df, split across a table, or written in a non-APA style are not extracted at all.

**Software.** R package `statcheck` (Nuijten maintainer, Epskamp), CRAN 1.5.0 (2024-02-16), dev 1.6.0 at github.com/MicheleNuijten/statcheck; doi:10.32614/cran.package.statcheck. Web app at statcheck.io (default options only). Python port at github.com/hplisiecki/statcheck_python — not on PyPI, **[unverified: whether it reproduces the R results]**.

**Prevalence findings.** Nuijten, M. B., Hartgerink, C. H. J., van Assen, M. A. L. M., Epskamp, S., & Wicherts, J. M. (2016). *The prevalence of statistical reporting errors in psychology (1985–2013)*. Behavior Research Methods, 48, 1205–1226. doi:10.3758/s13428-015-0664-2. Over 250,000 p-values, 8 journals, 1985–2013: **~50% of NHST-using papers had ≥1 inconsistent p-value; ~1 in 8 (12.5%) had a decision inconsistency.** Prevalence stable or slightly declining over the period.

**Precision and recall — three published positions, and they measure different things.**

1. *Conditional accuracy* (given a test was extracted, is the verdict right?). Nuijten, M. B., van Assen, M. A. L. M., Hartgerink, C. H. J., Epskamp, S., & Wicherts, J. M. (2017). *The validity of the tool "statcheck" in discovering statistical reporting inconsistencies*. PsyArXiv, doi:10.31234/osf.io/tcxaj (preprint only — no journal version exists). **Sensitivity 85.3–100%, specificity 96.0–100%, overall accuracy 96.2–99.9%**, range depending on assumptions and settings; interrater reliability with manual coding **.76–.89**.
2. *Unconditional accuracy* (over all tests present in the paper). Schmidt, T. (2017). *Statcheck does not work: All the numbers. Reply to Nuijten et al. (2017)*. PsyArXiv, doi:10.31234/osf.io/hr6qy. Same 49 papers. statcheck recognises only **61%** of the statistical tests present; recomputing over all 1,120 tests gives **29 hits, 19 false alarms, ~27 missed true inconsistencies, 435 tests never recognised → precision 60.4%, sensitivity 51.8%, phi = .54**, against a true-inconsistency base rate of ~5%. Substantive claim: statcheck cannot handle *corrected* p-values (multiple-comparison, Greenhouse–Geisser, post-hoc), so correctly reported corrected tests are flagged as errors. Earlier version: Schmidt, T. (2016). *Sources of false positives and false negatives in the STATCHECK algorithm*. arXiv:1610.01010.
3. *Coverage, measured empirically.* Böschen, I. (2021). *Evaluation of JATSdecoder as an automated text extraction tool for statistical results in scientific reports*. Scientific Reports, 11. doi:10.1038/s41598-021-98782-3. **This is the hardest quantitative evidence on statcheck's recall and it is the least cited.** On 49 manually coded articles: manual coding found 1,148 results; JATSdecoder `get.stats()` found 1,095; statcheck `checkHTML()` found **129**; statcheck `checkPDF()` found **0**. Across 56,005 articles: JATSdecoder 359,440 checkable results vs statcheck 112,936. Follow-up polemic: Böschen, I. (2024). *statcheck is flawed by design and no valid spell checker for statistical results*. arXiv:2408.07948 — 187 hand-constructed reporting-style variants; recommends JATSdecoder and LLMs instead. No precision/recall table.

**Resolution for a pipeline.** Both camps are right about different quantities. Report statcheck's *coverage* (what fraction of the paper's reported tests it parsed) alongside its flags, and treat a flag as ~60% likely to be a real inconsistency rather than ~96%. Never report a statcheck-derived error rate without stating the extraction denominator.

**Journal deployment — the strongest effect evidence.** Nuijten, M. B., & Wicherts, J. M. (2024). *Implementing statcheck during peer review is related to a steep decline in statistical-reporting inconsistencies*. AMPPS. doi:10.1177/25152459241258945. 8,814 articles, 147,784 results, pretest–posttest quasi-experiment. Statcheck journals (*Psychological Science* from July 2016, *JESP* from August 2017): inconsistencies **8.8% → 4.3%**, decision inconsistencies **1.2% → 0.3%**. Control journals (*JEP:General*, *JPSP*): 7.4% → 6.4% and 1.0% → 0.8%. Period × statcheck-journal interaction *b* = −0.71, 95% CI [−0.92, −0.51], *p* < .001 (OR 0.49).

**The 2016 PubPeer episode — the governance lesson.** Hartgerink ran statcheck over ~50,000 psychology papers and auto-posted reports to PubPeer, covering just under 700,000 statistical results; roughly half of the 16,695 analysed articles showed an inconsistency and ~12% a consequential one. The German Psychological Society issued a critical declaration on 2016-10-20 objecting to the disclosure mode and to irremovable false-positive flags. Sources: Retraction Watch (2016-09-02); DGPs letter. This is why RegCheck and metacheck both refuse to issue verdicts.

**Related uses.** Nuijten & Polanin (2020), Research Synthesis Methods, doi:10.1002/jrsm.1408; Polanin & Nuijten (2018), Campbell Systematic Reviews, doi:10.4073/csrm.2018.1. Pre-statcheck hand-coded baseline: Bakker & Wicherts (2011), Behavior Research Methods, doi:10.3758/s13428-011-0089-5.

**2026 development.** Böschen, I. (2026). *Extraction of tabulated statistical results with tableParser*. arXiv:2603.19756. R package extracting tables from JATS-XML, HTML, DOCX and (with limits) PDF; `table2stats()` returns detected results with a recalculated p-value. Directly addresses statcheck's blind spot for results reported in tables. Works best on JATS HTML; PDF table extraction often fails.

### 1.2 Granularity / distributional consistency tests (GRIM family)

These check whether reported summary statistics are *arithmetically possible* given N and item granularity. No raw data needed.

- **GRIM** — Brown, N. J. L., & Heathers, J. A. J. (2016/2017). *The GRIM test: A simple technique detects numerous anomalies in the reporting of results in psychology*. Social Psychological and Personality Science, 8(4), 363–369. doi:10.1177/1948550616673876 (online 2016-10-18); preprint doi:10.7287/peerj.preprints.2064v1. For integer-valued data, `n × mean` must be a whole number (adjusted for item count). Empirical result in the paper: of 260 recent articles in leading journals, **71 were GRIM-testable; ~half (36) contained at least one impossible mean and >20% (16) contained multiple**. Data were requested for 21, 9 responded, and **a reporting error was confirmed in all 9**.
  - Limits: needs integer/granular data, known N, known item count, fixed decimal precision. Power falls as N rises — with 2-decimal means the test has no power once N ≥ 100. A passing mean is uninformative. False positives come from missing data (reported N ≠ N the mean used), rounding conventions, and mis-stated item counts.
- **GRIMMER** — Anaya, J. (**2016**, not 2017). *The GRIMMER test: A method for testing the validity of reported measures of variability*. PeerJ Preprints, doi:10.7287/peerj.preprints.2400v1. No journal version.
- **Analytic-GRIMMER** — Allard, A. (2018), blog post at aurelienallard.netlify.app, **not a paper, no DOI** — but this is the algorithm `scrutiny` actually implements: rearrange the sample-variance formula so `(n−1)σ² + nμ²` equals the integer sum of squared responses, then run GRIM on the mean, bound the sum of squares given rounding, and check parity.
- **SPRITE** — Heathers, J. A. J., Anaya, J., van der Zee, T., & Brown, N. J. L. (2018). *Recovering data from summary statistics: SPRITE*. PeerJ Preprints, doi:10.7287/peerj.preprints.26968v1. Given mean, SD, N and scale range, stochastically searches for integer datasets consistent with those parameters. It is a **sampler** — it does not enumerate the solution space.
- **CORVIDS** — Wilner, S. A., Wood, K., & Simons, D. J. (2018). *Complete recovery of values in Diophantine systems*. PsyArXiv, doi:10.31234/osf.io/7shr8; code at github.com/katherinemwood/corvids. Solves the constraints as a Diophantine system and returns **all** solutions — provably complete, unlike SPRITE. Runtime grows steeply with scale range and N. **Preprint only; no journal version in Crossref or OpenAlex.**
- **RIVETS** — Brown & Heathers (2019). PsyArXiv, doi:10.31234/osf.io/ctu9z. Tests whether a reported test statistic is consistent with rounded input descriptives.
- **DEBIT** — Heathers & Brown (2019), OSF osf.io/5vb3u. **An OSF document, not a journal article.** For binary data the SD is a deterministic function of mean and N, so any deviation is an error.
- **BRIMMER** — Hussey, Jung, Lakens & Cummins (2026), bounds on the SD under constraints. PsyArXiv osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/4v7pe. **[unverified: not fetched]**
- Applications: van der Zee, Anaya & Brown (2017). *Statistical heartburn: an attempt to digest four pizza publications from the Cornell Food and Brand Lab*. BMC Nutrition, 3(1), 54. doi:10.1186/s40795-017-0167-x. Outside psychology: De Cassai et al. (2024), Canadian Journal of Anesthesia, doi:10.1007/s12630-024-02772-8.
- **[unverified] Bauer & Francis** methodological paper on GRIM/GRIMMER — only their *Psychological Science* Expressions of Concern were located (doi:10.1177/09567976211058727, doi:10.1177/09567976211035782).

**Implementations.**
- `scrutiny` (Lukas Jung), CRAN, doi:10.32614/cran.package.scrutiny, v0.6.1, MIT, docs at lhdjung.github.io/scrutiny. The complete R suite: `grim*` (incl. `grim_map_seq()` for dispersed inputs and `grim_map_total_n()` for hypothetical group sizes, `grim_probability()`, `grim_ratio()`, `grim_plot()`), `grimmer*` (Analytic-GRIMMER), `debit*`, duplication analysis (`duplicate_count()`, `duplicate_tally()`), an `audit()`/`audit_seq()`/`audit_total_n()` summary layer, rounding infrastructure, and scaffolding for user-defined consistency tests.
- `rsprite2` (Lukas Wallrich, with Aurélien Allard), CRAN 0.2.1 (2023-07-06). Extends SPRITE to multi-item Likert scales and frequency restrictions.
- `ERROR` R package (github.com/ianhussey/ERROR) bundles statcheck, GRIM, GRIMMER, SPRITE and effect-size recalculation. **No LLM component.**
- **Python coverage is essentially absent.** Only `grim` on PyPI (v0.1.10, Peter Houghton, github.com/phoughton/grim_test). No GRIMMER, DEBIT, SPRITE or CORVIDS package. R is the only complete implementation.

### 1.3 papercheck → **metacheck** (DeBruine & Lakens)

**The package has been renamed.** `scienceverse/papercheck` redirects to `scienceverse/metacheck`; `scienceverse.org/papercheck/` redirects to `scienceverse.org/metacheck/`. Anything written about "papercheck" should now target metacheck. Version 0.1.0, lifecycle experimental, AGPL-3.0. Install: `pak::pkg_install("scienceverse/metacheck")`. Authors: Lisa DeBruine (Glasgow), Daniël Lakens (Eindhoven), with Cristian Mesquida, Jakub Werner, Lavinia Ion, Hadeel Khawatmy, Levi Baruch, Mink Veltman; part of the Research Transparency Check project with René Bekkers (VU Amsterdam).

**Architecture worth copying.** A paper is parsed (Grobid PDF → TEI XML, or `bibr` JSON) into a **paper object** of sentence-level text plus tables and references. **Modules** are small independent checks run against that object. `report()` assembles module output into HTML. There are first-class `validate()` and `accuracy()` functions. The stated policy: checks assist, never replace, human expertise, and the project "will never support automated quality assessments, rankings or scoring." Modelled explicitly on CRAN checks for R packages.

**Modules (from the function reference and the manual at scienceverse.org/metacheck_book):**

| Group | Modules |
|---|---|
| General | `all_urls`, `coi_check`, `funding_check`, `open_practices`, ethics-approval check |
| Method | `causal_claims` (is random assignment reported? list causal-claim sentences), `power` (regex + optional LLM), `prereg_check`, **preregistration comparison via RegCheck** |
| Results | `all_p_values`, `stat_check` (statcheck-equivalent), `stat_effect_size`, `stat_p_exact`, `stat_p_nonsig`, `marginal`, `code_check`, `repo_check`, data check, data-quality checks, codebook check, Psych-DS check, JASP/jamovi/SPSS import, reproducibility check |
| References | `ref_accuracy` (CrossRef), `ref_consistency`, `ref_miscitation`, `ref_pubpeer`, `ref_replication` (FLoRA), `ref_retraction` (Retraction Watch), `ref_summary` |

**Other reusable infrastructure:** `llm()` / `llm_model()` / `llm_max_calls()` (LLM calls with an explicit call budget) including **local models via Ollama**; OSF / GitHub / Zenodo / ResearchBox / AsPredicted link-finding and file download; `causal_relations()` (Hugging Face Space for causal-relation extraction); `code_parse_r()` / `code_read()` / `code_extract_r()`; the `psychsci` corpus (Psychological Science open-access papers) as a test set.

**Two gaps.** No preprint or paper describing metacheck is indexed in Crossref or OpenAlex. **No published accuracy or validation evaluation was found for any module** — a significant gap given the module count, and notable given the package ships `validate()` and `accuracy()`.

**Conceptual foundation:** Lakens, D., & DeBruine, L. M. (2021). *Improving transparency, falsifiability, and rigor by making hypothesis tests machine-readable*. AMPPS. doi:10.1177/2515245920970949.

### 1.5 RegCheck — registration vs. paper consistency

Cummins, J., Clarke, B., Hussey, I., & Elson, M. (2026). *RegCheck: A tool for structured comparisons between study registrations and papers*. arXiv:2601.13330 (submitted 2026-01-19, revised 2026-07-13, cs.CL, 26 pp., CC BY 4.0). App at regcheck.app; code at github.com/JamieCummins/regcheck (AGPL-3.0, v1.0.0). Psychology of Digitalisation lab, University of Bern (same lab as ERROR), plus the Bennett Institute, Oxford (Clarke). SNSF-funded.

**Six-stage "Parity Engine":** ingestion (registry link, file, or repository; PDF via GROBID/DPT-2) → preparation (strips references, filters multi-study noise) → semantic chunking and embedding → user-chosen comparison dimensions → retrieval-augmented extraction and LLM adjudication into **Deviation / No deviation / Insufficient evidence** → shareable report with a unique RegCheck ID.

**Reported accuracy: none, deliberately.** The paper states it does "not yet provide guarantees about the fidelity of RegCheck's deviation judgements" and advises users to verify all output while systematic evaluation is ongoing.

**Its evaluation design is the most transferable idea in this whole scan.** The authors explicitly reject a ground-truth framing and target **relative non-inferiority to observed human–human interrater agreement**, on both extracted text and consistency judgements, across psychology, clinical medicine and economics. Every judgement is backed by verbatim excerpts with cosine-similarity relevance scores, so a human can check the model against the source. No independent evaluation exists — the tool is seven months old.

**Downstream:** wrapped as the `reg_check` module in metacheck. **Extension:** Cummins, Drysdale, Elson, Hussey, Goldacre & DeVito (2026). *Automating clinical trial outcome identification and misreporting detection using RegCheck*. medRxiv, doi:10.64898/2026.07.30.26358885 (Goldacre/DeVito bring the COMPare outcome-switching agenda).

**Why the problem is hard, and why non-inferiority is the defensible target:**
- Claesen, A., Gomes, S., Tuerlinckx, F., & Vanpaemel, W. (2021). *Comparing dream to reality*. Royal Society Open Science, 8(10). doi:10.1098/rsos.211037. 27 Preregistered-badge studies in *Psychological Science*: **2 of 27 had no deviations; 1 disclosed all; 9 disclosed none.**
- van den Akker, O., van Assen, M., Enting, M., de Jonge, M., et al. (2023). *Selective hypothesis reporting in psychology*. AMPPS. doi:10.1177/25152459231187988. 459 preregistered studies: **52% omitted hypotheses, 57% added hypotheses**; ~1 in 5 changed direction.
- Bakker, M., Veldkamp, C. L. S., van Assen, M., Crompvoets, E., et al. (2020). *Ensuring the quality and specificity of preregistrations*. PLOS Biology, 18(12): e3000937. doi:10.1371/journal.pbio.3000937. **Human coder concordance on the number of hypotheses was only 14%.** This is the practical reason ground truth is unavailable for hypothesis-level checking.
- Torka, A.-K., & Hüffmeier, J. (2025). *Going off script*. Journal of Occupational and Organizational Psychology, 98(4). doi:10.1111/joop.70057. Only **0.70%** of published I/O studies preregistered; of 25 analysed, **72% deviated and 68% had ≥1 undisclosed deviation**.
- COMPare (Goldacre et al., compare-trials.org): 67 trials in top-five medical journals, **9 perfectly reported, 301 prespecified outcomes unreported, 357 silently added.**

### 1.6 The ERROR project (human-expert baseline)

error.reviews — a "bug bounty program for science" run from Malte Elson's Psychology of Digitalisation group, University of Bern, with Ian Hussey (Bern) and Ruben Arslan (Leipzig). Stated payout pool 250,000 CHF.

- Elson, M. (2024). *Pay researchers to spot errors in published papers*. Nature, 629, 730. doi:10.1038/d41586-024-01465-y. **Crossref lists Elson as sole author** — this is often miscited with co-authors and often under the title "A Bug Bounty Program for Science."
- Nowogrodzki, J. (2024). *Cash for errors: project offers bounty for spotting mistakes in published papers*. Nature news. doi:10.1038/d41586-024-02681-2.
- *Offering scientists cash to spot errors in published papers doesn't work*. Science news (2026). doi:10.1126/science.zh9l2q0.

**Bounty structure** (error.reviews/participation; amounts scaled for regional cost differences):

| Outcome | Author receives | Reviewer bonus | Recommended action |
|---|---|---|---|
| No errors | 250 CHF | — | Publish error report + reply |
| Minor errors | 250 CHF | 250 CHF | Publish report/reply + acknowledgment |
| Indeterminable | — | 250 CHF | Note lack of verifiability |
| Moderate errors | — | 500 CHF | Minor correction notice |
| Major errors | — | 1,000 CHF | Major correction notice / expression of concern |
| Severe errors | — | 2,500 CHF | Retraction |

Base pay: authors 250 CHF for participating; reviewers 250–1,000 CHF by expected effort. The author is paid *more* if no or only minor errors are found; the reviewer is paid more as severity rises.

**Throughput.** Target: 100 in-depth critiques over four years. The 2026 *Science* piece reports **nine completed and eight in progress, of which one contained no errors** — the rest had errors, several minor. The project's public completed-reviews page listed **six** at the time of this scan (Fernbach et al. 2019; Lades et al. 2020; Hehman et al. 2018; Cikara et al. 2014; Joel et al. 2017; Wessel 2018), so the counts differ by source and date. **No aggregate error rate has been published.** The binding constraint is reviewer recruitment, not error supply: candidates decline, agree then stall, or stop replying.

**Design lesson.** Expert human reanalysis does not scale on volunteer or modest-bounty economics. That is the gap an automated pipeline addresses — and it means the pipeline's own measured precision and recall is a first-class deliverable, not an afterthought.

**Related outputs from the same group** (relevant as distributional-anomaly detection in the GRIM spirit): Hussey, I., Alsalti, T., Bosco, F., Elson, M., & Arslan, R. (2025). *An aberrant abundance of Cronbach's alpha values at .70*. AMPPS. doi:10.1177/25152459241287123. Also Anvari, Alsalti, Oehler, Hussey et al. (2025), *Defragmenting psychology*, Nature Human Behaviour, doi:10.1038/s41562-025-02138-0, and *A fragmented field*, AMPPS, doi:10.1177/25152459251360642.

- Elliott, Kudrin & Wüthrich (2025). *The power of tests for detecting p-hacking*. Review of Economics and Statistics. doi:10.1162/rest.a.1688. Deliberately negative result: power "can be low and depends crucially on the p-hacking strategy and the distribution of true effects."

- Head, M. L., Holman, L., Lanfear, R., Kahn, A. T., & Jennions, M. D. (2015). *The extent and consequences of p-hacking in science*. PLOS Biology, 13(3): e1002106. doi:10.1371/journal.pbio.1002106.
- Bishop, D. V. M., & Thompson, P. A. (2016). *Problems in using p-curve analysis and text-mining to detect rate of p-hacking and evidential value*. PeerJ, 4: e1715. doi:10.7717/peerj.1715. **The critique any corpus pipeline must answer:** (1) "ghost variable" p-hacking — selectively reporting the significant DV among several — does **not** reliably produce a p ≈ .05 bump, especially with uncorrelated DVs; (2) indiscriminate text-mining sweeps in manipulation checks and method validation, so the extracted p-distribution is not the one the method assumes.
- Stefan, A. M., & Schönbrodt, F. D. (2023). *Big little lies: a compendium and simulation of p-hacking strategies*. Royal Society Open Science, 10(2). doi:10.1098/rsos.220346. **Catalogues 12 p-hacking strategies and simulates the false-positive inflation of each.** Evaluate any detector per strategy against this set, not against a generic model.

**HARKing: no dedicated text-mining method or tool exists.** The literature attacks it through manual preregistration↔paper comparison; RegCheck's hypothesis dimension is the only automated attempt found. Bakker et al.'s 14% coder concordance on hypothesis counts explains the absence.

| **Reasoning LLMs on withdrawn papers** — Zhang, T. M., & Abernethy, N. F. | **Google Paper Assistant Tool** — Jayaram, R., Tyler, D., Woodruff, D., Cortes, C., et al. (2026). arXiv:2606.28277 | Applied to SPOT | **+34% over zero-shot recall on SPOT mathematical errors** — relative to a very low base. Absolute figures **[unverified]** |

**The pattern is consistent across three years.** On real errors from the wild, LLMs are near-useless (≤21% recall, ≤6% precision — about 15 false alarms per true find). On injected errors they do better but still fail most of the time. When the flaw is in *reasoning* rather than in a number, they do not notice at all. Targeted single-purpose checks beat holistic review, in every study that compared them.

### 2.2 Can LLMs judge whether the statistical model fits the question and data?

**This is the thinnest part of the entire evidence base. No benchmark isolates statistical-model appropriateness as a scored category.**

- The one direct study is a ceiling effect: Shukla, M., Pandey, D., Kaur, S., Agarwal, M., Goyal, A., & Sharma, H. (2025). *Evaluating the accuracy and explanatory quality of large language models … in statistical test selection for hypothesis testing decisions*. Cureus. doi:10.7759/cureus.94949. 20 standardised scenarios; **all six models (ChatGPT, Claude, DeepSeek, Gemini, Grok, Le Chat) scored 100%.** Treat as evidence about clean vignettes, not manuscripts — no distractors, design stated explicitly, no false-positive measurement, no case where nesting is implicit or the DV was dichotomised three pages earlier.
- Zhu, Y., Ding, Y., Lai, P., Wang, L., Jing, B., & Chen, G. (2026). *StatABench: Dataset and framework for evaluating statistical analysis capabilities of LLMs*. arXiv:2606.22977. Stat-Closed: 404 questions over 18 statistical topics; Stat-Open: 30 open-ended modelling tasks. **GPT-5.1 reaches only 68.6% on Stat-Closed**; best open-source 60.6%; best agent framework 61.86 on Stat-Open. Failure modes explicitly include **methodological decision-making**. This is the closest direct evidence that "is this the right analysis?" remains weak.

### 2.3 LLMs as peer reviewers — the general evidence

- **Liang, W., Zhang, Y., Cao, H., Wang, B., Ding, D. Y., et al., & Zou, J. (2024).** *Can large language models provide useful feedback on research papers? A large-scale empirical analysis*. NEJM AI, 1(8). doi:10.1056/AIoa2400196; arXiv:2310.01783. GPT-4/human comment overlap **30.85%** (Nature-family, n = 3,096) and **39.23%** (ICLR, n = 1,709); human/human overlap 28.58% and 35.25%. Prospective study, 308 researchers at 110 US institutions: **57.4%** found the feedback helpful or very helpful; **82.4%** found it more useful than feedback from at least some human reviewers. **Caveat for this project: this measures overlap with generic reviewer comments, not detection of errors.** The §2.1 benchmarks test that directly and the answer is different.
- **Thakkar, N., Yuksekgonul, M., Silberg, J., Garg, A., et al. (2025).** *Can LLM feedback enhance review quality? A randomized study of 20K reviews at ICLR 2025*. arXiv:2504.09737. The strongest field evidence, and note the design: **the LLM critiques human reviews, it does not replace them.** 22,467 reviews randomly selected, 18,946 received feedback; **5,031 (26.6%) were updated**, incorporating 12,222 feedback items; updated reviews grew ~80 words; >96% of generated feedback passed automated reliability checks.
- **Biswas, J., Schoepp, S., Vasan, G., Opipari, A., et al. (2026).** *AI-assisted peer review at scale: the AAAI-26 AI review pilot*. arXiv:2604.13940. Every main-track submission got one labelled AI review: **22,977 papers in under 24 hours.** Survey (5,834 responses): **53.9%** judged AI reviews useful vs 20.2% who disagreed. AI rated above human on six of nine quality dimensions, largest gaps on identifying technical errors (**+0.67**) and raising unconsidered points (**+0.61**). Symmetric split: 46.6% agreed AI caught concerns humans would miss; 49.4% agreed AI missed points humans would catch.
- **Kim, S., Yoon, D., Gashteovski, K., Suk, J., et al. (2026).** *On the limits and opportunities of AI reviewers: reviewing the reviews of Nature-family papers with 45 expert scientists*. arXiv:2605.20668. **The single most design-relevant result.** 45 domain experts, 469 hours, 2,960 individual criticisms across 82 Nature-family papers. GPT-5.2 scored above each paper's top-rated human reviewer (**60.0% vs 48.2%, p = 0.009**) and raised **26%** of issues no human raised. But **AI–AI reviewer overlap was 21% vs human–human 3%** — adding more AI reviewers buys far less independent signal than adding humans. Sixteen recurring weaknesses humans do not share (thin subfield knowledge, poor multi-document long-context handling, over-criticism of trivia).
- **Du, J., Wang, Y., Zhao, W., Deng, et al. (2024).** *LLMs assist NLP researchers: critique paper (meta-)reviewing*. EMNLP 2024. doi:10.18653/v1/2024.emnlp-main.292; arXiv:2406.16253. Introduces **ReviewCritique** with expert per-segment deficiency labels. The authors state LLMs are "currently inadequate for tasks that require a high level of expertise" and explicitly do not advocate LLM (meta-)reviewing.
- **Ou, J., Gantt Walden, W., Sanders, K., et al. (2025).** *CLAIMCHECK: How grounded are LLM critiques of scientific papers?* arXiv:2503.21717. Built from NeurIPS 2023/2024 OpenReview data. Three tasks: associate a review weakness with the claim it disputes; predict fine-grained weakness labels and rewrite for specificity; verify claims with grounded reasoning. **Frontier LLMs match humans only on label prediction/rewriting and underperform experts on claim–weakness association and grounded verification.** The closest benchmark to "does the critique attach to the right claim."
- **Zhou, R., Chen, L., & Yu, K. (2024).** *Is LLM a reliable reviewer?* LREC-COLING 2024, 9340–9351, aclanthology.org/2024.lrec-main.816/. 197 review-revision items from ICLR 2023: GPT-3.5/GPT-4 exceed 60% on single options but only ~**20% fully correct**.
- **Baumann, J., Pei, J., Koyejo, S., & Hovy, D. (2026).** *Stop automating peer review without rigorous evaluation*. arXiv:2605.03202 (ICML 2026 Position Paper, oral). Documents a "hivemind effect" — excessive agreement within and across papers — and shows AI review scores move on **stylistic** changes rather than scientific merit.

### 2.4 Sycophancy, score inflation, gameability

- **The AI Review Lottery** — Russo Latona, G., Horta Ribeiro, M., Davidson, T. R., et al. arXiv:2405.02150; Proc. ACM Hum.-Comput. Interact., doi:10.1145/3757667 **[unverified — ACM 403]**. ≥**15.8%** of ICLR 2024 reviews AI-assisted; in **53.4%** of paired comparisons the AI-assisted review scored higher; borderline submissions receiving one were **+4.9 percentage points** more likely to be accepted (p = 0.024).

- **Li, L., Xiong, J., Zhu, C., Yu, R., et al. (2026).** *LLM-as-a-reviewer*. arXiv:2605.25415. 12 LLMs, 898 papers: systematic over-rating of weak submissions; reviews 2–3× longer than human with lower lexical diversity; **under-flag clarity, over-flag reproducibility**; hidden injections lift low-scoring papers to acceptance level.
- **Ye, R., Pang, X., Chai, J., et al. (2024).** *Are we there yet? Revealing the risks of utilizing LLMs in scholarly peer review*. arXiv:2412.01708. Tampering with **5%** of reviews moved **12%** of papers out of the top 30%. Higher ratings for *incomplete* papers than complete ones.

- **Goldberg, A., Ullah, I., Khuong, T. G. H., et al. (2024).** *Usefulness of LLMs as an author checklist assistant: NeurIPS'24 experiment*. arXiv:2411.03417. 234 papers; >70% of authors found it useful; **gameable** — fabricated justifications raised scores.

## 3. Multiverse / specification-curve tooling

### 3.1 Method foundations

- Steegen, S., Tuerlinckx, F., Gelman, A., & Vanpaemel, W. (2016). *Increasing transparency through a multiverse analysis*. Perspectives on Psychological Science. doi:10.1177/1745691616658637
- Simonsohn, U., Simmons, J. P., & Nelson, L. D. (2020). *Specification curve analysis*. Nature Human Behaviour. doi:10.1038/s41562-020-0912-z (correction doi:10.1038/s41562-020-00974-w; 2015 working paper doi:10.2139/ssrn.2694998). Three steps: enumerate reasonable specifications, plot the descriptive curve, then test against a permutation null. **The inferential step is what most applied uses omit.**
- Young, C., & Holsteen, K. (2017). *Model uncertainty and robustness: a computational framework for multimodel analysis*. Sociological Methods & Research. doi:10.1177/0049124115610347
- Slez, A. (2019). *The difference between instability and uncertainty: comment on Young and Holsteen (2017)*. Sociological Methods & Research. doi:10.1177/0049124117729704. **Variation across specifications is *instability*, not *uncertainty*; treating the modelling distribution as a sampling distribution is a category error.** The cleanest statement of what a specification curve does and does not license.
- Young, C., & Cumberworth, E. (2025). *Multiverse Analysis*. Cambridge University Press. doi:10.1017/9781009003391. Book-length treatment.
- Patel, C. J., Burford, B., & Ioannidis, J. P. A. (2015). *Assessment of vibration of effects due to model specification can demonstrate the instability of observational associations*. Journal of Clinical Epidemiology. doi:10.1016/j.jclinepi.2015.05.029. 417 NHANES variables × 8,192 Cox models each. **For 31% of variables the association sign flipped between the 99th and 1st percentile of the model distribution (a "Janus effect").** Extension to sampling and measurement uncertainty: Klau, Hoffmann, Patel, Ioannidis & Boulesteix (2020), International Journal of Epidemiology, doi:10.1093/ije/dyaa164.
- Del Giudice, M., & Gangestad, S. W. (2021). *A traveler's guide to the multiverse: promises, pitfalls, and a framework for the evaluation of analytic decisions*. AMPPS. doi:10.1177/2515245920954925. **The key conceptual correction: specifications are not exchangeable.** Introduces **Type E** (principled equivalence), **Type N** (principled non-equivalence) and **Type U** (uncertain) decisions — **only Type U decisions belong in a multiverse.** Where alternatives are not arbitrary, a multiverse can hide a real effect "within a mass of poorly justified alternatives." An automated specification generator needs this taxonomy or it produces meaningless dispersion.
- Cantone, G. G., & Tomaselli, V. (2024). *Characterisation and calibration of multiversal methods*. Advances in Data Analysis and Classification, 19(4), 989–1021. doi:10.1007/s11634-024-00610-9. The formal treatment of **specification weighting**: Bayesian model averaging reduces error for well-specified multiverses but **amplifies error when a collider sits in the model space**, and does not beat uniform weighting for well-specified models. Do not add weighting as a safety net.
- Harder, J. A. (2020). *The multiverse of methods: extending the multiverse analysis to address data-collection decisions*. Perspectives on Psychological Science. doi:10.1177/1745691620917678
- Girardi, P., Vesely, A., Lakens, D., Altoè, G., Pastore, M., et al. (2024). *Post-selection inference in multiverse analysis (PIMA): an inferential framework based on the sign-flipping score test*. Psychometrika. doi:10.1007/s11336-024-09973-6. Gives a valid global test across a multiverse — the natural inferential backend for an automated pipeline.
- Klau, S., Schönbrodt, F., Patel, C., Ioannidis, J., & Boulesteix, A.-L. (2023). *Comparing the vibration of effects due to model, data pre-processing and sampling uncertainty*. Meta-Psychology. doi:10.15626/mp.2020.2556
- Short, C., Inceler, Y., Frank, M., & Hildebrandt, A. (2025). *The Systematic Multiverse Analysis Registration Tool (SMART) for defining multiverse analyses*. Royal Society Open Science. doi:10.1098/rsos.250800. A structured schema for *declaring* a multiverse before running it. Useful as the interchange format between a specification generator and an execution engine.
- Short, C., Breznau, N., Bruntsch, M., … Hildebrandt, A. (2026). *Multicurious: A multidisciplinary guide to multiverse analysis*. AMPPS, 9(2). doi:10.1177/25152459261434881. 18 authors; cross-disciplinary guidance on defensibility and equivalence evaluation, preregistration, and computational cost. **The current practical reference.**
- Aczel, B., Szaszi, B., Nilsonne, G., et al. (2021). *Consensus-based guidance for conducting and reporting multi-analyst studies*. eLife. doi:10.7554/eLife.72185
- Nepomuceno, A., Ghosal, A., Sandoval-Lentisco, A., & Ioannidis, J. P. A. (2026). *Uptake and implementation of multiverse-style analyses across 613 studies*. bioRxiv, doi:10.64898/2026.07.15.738584. Preprint, not peer reviewed.

**The live disagreement about what a multiverse is for.** This matters because it determines what the pipeline should *output*.

- Ganslmeier, M., & Vlandas, T. (2025). *Estimating the extent and sources of model uncertainty in political science*. PNAS, 122(25). doi:10.1073/pnas.2414926122. Over **3.6 billion estimates** across four literatures; widespread uncertainty in significance *and* sign.
- Auspurg, K. (2025). *Robustness is better assessed with a few thoughtful models than with billions of regressions*. PNAS, 122(43). doi:10.1073/pnas.2521917122. Reply: doi:10.1073/pnas.2523374122.
- Modrák, M. (2026). *Multiverse analysis, abdication of responsibility and manufacturing of doubt*. arXiv:2607.14623. The sharpest recent attack: multiverse analysis suits two undesirable uses — avoiding responsibility for a conclusion, and manufacturing doubt. Proposes two conventions: **evaluate a multiverse by its worst case, and treat a large multiverse as a sign of weakness, not rigour.** Preprint.
- Rohrer, J., Hullman, J., & Gelman, A. (2026). *What's a multiverse good for anyway?* PsyArXiv, doi:10.31234/osf.io/37g29. Separates the multiverse as a tool for reflection, as persuasion, and as inference. It **fails inferentially "when the included analyses do not target a coherent estimand."** Preprint.
- Krähmer & Young (2026, below) report a hurricane-fatalities case where **98.7% of defensible specifications contradict the original finding**, and argue specification curves degrade into unreadable fuzz past ~300 estimates.

**Name traps.** The R `metaverse` project is evidence synthesis for systematic reviews, unrelated. `cSEM` is composite-SEM estimation with no multiverse machinery. `spec_curve` and `specurve` do not exist as R packages. **There is no Python `multiverse` by Sarma — that package is R-only.** Stata `speccheck` and `multishell` were not found. No Julia package found.

**Computational shortcuts — a short list, because scaling is an open problem.**
1. **Prefix pruning** in `multiverse` (Sarma et al., CHI 2023, doi:10.1145/3544548.3580726): universes share pipeline prefixes, so shared intermediate steps compute once. **The only shipped shared-computation mechanism found.** Plus future-backed parallelism and per-universe `execute_universe()`.
2. Liu, Althoff & Heer, *Approximation and progressive display of multiverse analyses*, CHI 2023, arXiv:2305.08323 — sampling and progressive visualisation to monitor a multiverse mid-execution.
3. Sarma, Hwang, Hullman & Kay, *Milliways*, CHI 2024 — separates probabilistic from possibilistic (choice) uncertainty. **No released software artefact located.**
4. Bell, Kampman, Dodge & Lawrence, *Modeling the machine learning multiverse*, arXiv:2206.05985 — Gaussian-process surrogate plus Bayesian experimental design to explore a space without exhaustive enumeration. A paper, not a tool.
5. RobustiPy (Valdenegro, Yan, Dai, Rahal), arXiv:2506.19958 — Python library adding model selection, averaging, resampling and explainable-AI components.

**No surrogate/emulator package, no principled multiverse-sampling package, and no incremental refitting beyond `multiverse`'s prefix pruning exists.**

**Bayesian settings: no dedicated package.** The `multiverse` DSL is model-agnostic so `brm()` can sit inside a `branch()`, but none of its nine vignettes mention Stan or brms. The nearest maintained adjacent tool is `priorsense` (CRAN 1.2.0, 2025-10-28; Kallioinen, Paananen, Bürkner, Vehtari) — power-scaling prior/likelihood sensitivity, a **continuous perturbation diagnostic that avoids refitting a grid**. The obstacle is cost: a grid of MCMC fits.

**The enumeration gap — the most actionable finding in this scan.** **Nothing automatically derives decision points from existing analysis code.** Every tool requires hand annotation: the DSLs (`multiverse`, `boba`, `rdfanalysis`) need explicit `branch()`/template markup, and SMART has users type nodes into a web form. No static-analysis, code-transformation or LLM-based system ingests an analysis script and proposes its forking paths. LLMs are being used to *run* alternative analyses, not to *read a script and surface its decisions*.

**Visualisation at scale.** Krähmer, D., & Young, C. (2026). *Visualizing vastness: graphical methods for multiverse analysis*. PLOS ONE. doi:10.1371/journal.pone.0339452. Critiques specification curves (which need distorting sampling at scale) and density plots (which lose the decision structure), and proposes **multiverse plots**: a density summary of the modelling distribution plus a binned decision dashboard, scaling to millions of specifications. Directly relevant if the pipeline generates large automated specification spaces.

### 3.3b Reproducibility and robustness at scale (the Institute for Replication line)

Brodeur, A., Mikola, D., Cook, N., Fiala, L., et al. (2026). *Reproducibility and robustness of economics and political science research*. Nature, 652, 151–156. doi:10.1038/s41586-026-10251-x. 110 economics and political-science articles: **>85% computationally reproducible; 72% of significant estimates survived robustness checks; median reproduced effect = 99% of the published effect.** This is the peer-reviewed counterweight to the many-analysts pessimism: when the *same* analysis is rerun and then perturbed within the authors' own framing, most results hold.

### 3.4 LLM/agent multiverses — the 2026 core literature

This is the most directly relevant body of work and it is very recent.

- **Miao, J., Pritchard, J. K., & Zou, J. (2026). *The agentic garden of forking paths*. arXiv:2607.01507.** The most directly relevant work found, on both halves of the question. Across four high-stakes domains, **assigning different personas alone** is sufficient for AI agents to reach opposing conclusions from the same data and question, aligned with the assigned beliefs. On the Breznau immigration data (42 human teams), agents reproduced **72% of the human ideological gap** in reported effect estimates. **86% of the mutually contradictory analyses passed independent AI review and 78% passed majority human expert review** — peer review does not filter this. Introduces the **m-value (multiverse value)**: the probability that an analysis path would produce a claim at least as extreme as the reported one — a p-value analogue over the *analysis space* rather than the sampling distribution — estimated by an **Agentic Bootstrap** that samples plausible analysis paths. Applied to the human immigration study, **13.5% of reported human analyses fell in the most extreme 5%** (m < 0.05). Their framing is worth adopting: the problem is not flawed analyses but **selective exploration and reporting** from a large space of defensible ones, and agents make that exploration cheap.
- **Bertran, M., Fogliato, R., & Wu, Z. S. (2026). *Many AI analysts, one dataset: navigating the agentic data science multiverse*. PNAS. doi:10.1073/pnas.2606495123; preprint arXiv:2602.18710.** Autonomous LLM analysts each run a full pipeline on the same dataset and hypothesis, with a separate **auditor agent** validating methodological soundness. ~5,000 runs; **3,303 (67%) passed auditor compliance screening**. Datasets: Silberzahn red cards, METR-RCT (AI assistance and coding speed), ANES-Views. Models: Claude Sonnet 4.5, Claude Haiku 4.5, Qwen3 Coder 480B, Qwen3 235B. **Outcomes are steerable: the hypothesis-support gap between the most sceptical and most confirmation-seeking persona ranged from 34 percentage points (ANES) to 66 points (METR-RCT) — among methodologically sound runs.** Recommends multiverse-style reporting and **full prompt disclosure** as a norm equivalent to code and data sharing.
- **Alizadeh, M., Gilardi, F., Mosleh, M., & Kasneci, E. (2026). *AI coding agents in social science: methodologically diverse, empirically consistent, interpretively vulnerable*. arXiv:2606.11456.** 20 independent executions of Claude Code and Codex on the immigration study, against the human many-analysts baseline. **Design layer:** Codex matched human methodological diversity; Claude Code produced nearly 3× as many specifications; no agent model exactly matched any human model. **Estimate layer:** both agents' estimates stayed broadly aligned with human consensus, and a biased-researcher prompt reorganised methodological choices but — unlike with human analysts — did *not* shift aggregate estimates or verdicts. **Verdict layer: a confirmatory prompt flipped Claude Code's verdicts from 10% to 90% support without changing the coefficient distributions.** Their conclusion: "the locus of AI bias is not estimation but interpretation."

**An unresolved tension worth knowing about.** Miao et al. find agents *reproduce* human ideological dispersion in the estimates; Alizadeh et al. find agent estimates stay stable and only the *verdict* moves. Both used the Breznau immigration data. A pipeline should therefore instrument the estimate and the verdict separately — they do not move together.

**A validity caveat on LLM analyst crowds.** The generic literature consistently finds LLM outputs are *more homogeneous* than human populations: diversity collapse in multi-agent idea generation (arXiv:2604.18005), semantic convergence across prompting strategies (arXiv:2605.17193), and collapse embedded in post-training data composition so inference-time tuning does not close the gap (arXiv:2601.06116) — **[unverified: these three IDs were not independently fetched]**. This matters because both studies above obtain dispersion largely by **varying personas and prompts**, not because agents spontaneously disagree. **Whether persona-induced spread is a valid proxy for genuine researcher degrees of freedom is not settled by any dedicated paper found in this scan.**

- Ye, A., Huang, J. Y., Guo, A., Novick, R., Broderick, T., & Gordon, M. (2026). *Navigating the conceptual multiverse*. arXiv:2604.17815. Applies multiverse logic to the hidden **conceptual** decisions LLMs make on open-ended problems, with an interactive system users can inspect and intervene on, plus a verification framework enforcing unambiguity and completeness of the decision structure.
- Fa, D., & Culjak, M. (2026). *Sound agentic science requires adversarial experiments*. arXiv:2604.22080; ICLR 2026 Workshop on Agents in the Wild. Agents make it cheap to generate plausible, easily-revised analyses favouring publishable results; argues for a **falsification-first** standard.
- **Rewolinski, Z. T., Zane, A. V., Huang, H., Singh, C., Wang, C., Gao, J., & Yu, B. (2026). *Sanity checks for agentic data science*. arXiv:2604.11003.** Two cheap perturbation checks (grounded in the Predictability–Computability–Stability framework) testing whether an agent can distinguish signal from noise. On 11 real datasets with OpenAI Codex, **6 produced affirmative conclusions that lacked proper support**; agent self-reported confidence was **poorly calibrated** to empirical stability.
- **Linde, M., Sun, J., Balluff, P., Radovanović, D., & Chan, C. (2026). *Making uncertainty visible: multiverse analysis for robust computational social science*. arXiv:2605.19745.** Three case studies (Bayesian, network, ML). Under-reported contribution: **computational failure across specifications**. Some LLMs (Teuken, GPT-OSS, LLaMA3) failed to obey output formats and produced unparseable text; only Gemma3 kept failure below 1% across all specifications. Switching LLM changes the estimate — **LLM-as-measurement-instrument is itself a multiverse dimension.** Their specification curves stratify estimates by failure rate.
- **Gu, K., Shang, R., Jiang, R., et al., Heer, J., & Althoff, T. (2024). *BLADE: benchmarking language model agents for data-driven science*. EMNLP 2024, arXiv:2408.09667.** 12 datasets and research questions with ground truth from **independent expert analyses**, covering variable selection, transformations and model choice. LLM agents "possess considerable world knowledge but are often limited to basic analyses"; data access helps but they still underperform the expert decision set. The natural evaluation harness for an LLM specification generator.
- **Zeng, Q., Jin, C., Wang, X., Zheng, Y., & Li, Q. (2025). *AIRepr: an analyst–inspector framework for evaluating reproducibility of LLMs in data science*. EMNLP 2025 Findings, arXiv:2502.16395.** 15 analyst–inspector LLM pairs, 1,032 tasks. Workflows with higher reproducibility also yield more accurate analyses.

**The head-to-head benchmark: LLM reanalysts vs human reanalysts.**

**Holtdirk, T., Marcolongo, P., Steinberg Schulten, A., Henninger, F., Rose, S., Ball, S., Ma, B., Kreuter, F., Weinmann, M., & Feuerriegel, S. (2026). *Automated reproducibility assessments in the social and behavioral sciences using large language models*. arXiv:2606.13670 (v2, 2026-06-25); also under review, doi:10.21203/rs.3.rs-10313775/v1.** N = 180 published studies with predefined claims; an LLM pipeline reruns the analysis. 11 studies produced no viable effect-size estimate. Of the rest: **80% reached the same qualitative conclusion as the original; 24% recovered the original effect size within ±0.05 Cohen's d.** On the subset with a human comparison, the LLM matched the original conclusion in **95% of cases (humans 83%)** and recovered the effect size in **40% (humans 28%)**.

Multi100 uses the **same ±0.05 Cohen's d tolerance**, which makes the two directly comparable:

| | Same qualitative conclusion | Effect recovered within ±0.05 d |
|---|---|---|
| **Multi100** — human reanalysts, 100 studies (Nature 2026) | 74% | **34%** |
| **Holtdirk** — LLM pipeline, 180 studies | 80% | **24%** |
| **Holtdirk** — LLM, subset with human comparison | **95%** (humans 83%) | **40%** (humans 28%) |

**Read this the right way.** The headline is not that agents are unreliable. It is that **human reanalysts recover the original effect size only about a third of the time**, and the LLM figure sits in the same band. An agentic reanalysis pipeline should be benchmarked against the *human reanalysis rate*, not against the original paper.

**The upstream constraint.** Saju, L., Holtdirk, T., Mangroliya, M. P., & Bleier, A. (2025). *Computational reproducibility of R code supplements on OSF*. arXiv:2505.21590. 296 R projects, 264 retrieved. **98.8% lacked the formal dependency descriptions needed to execute.** After automated environment reconstruction into Docker containers, only **25.87% ran to completion without error.** Undeclared dependencies, invalid file paths and system-level issues dominate. Any reproduction pipeline spends most of its failure budget here, before any statistics are checked.

- **Siegel, Z. S., Kapoor, S., Nadgir, N., Stroebl, B., & Narayanan, A. (2025). *CORE-Bench*. Structured method extraction, strict information isolation, deterministic **cell-level** comparison against published tables, and error attribution separating agent error from paper underspecification. 48 human-verified papers, 4 scaffolds × 4 LLMs. Agents "largely recover published results," varying by model, scaffold and paper. Per-cell rates **[unverified]**. **This is the closest existing design to a social-science reproduction pipeline.**
- **Li, S., Wei, Q. A., Tang, J., Chen, V., et al. **The architecture that actually works: separate scientific reasoning from computational execution.**

**Xu (Stanford) & Yang (HKBU) (2026). *Scaling reproducibility: an AI-assisted workflow for large-scale reanalysis*.** Dated 2026-02-17; PDF at leoyang.org/publication/ai_reproducibility/example.pdf. **[unverified: no DOI or arXiv ID; read from the author's PDF]**. ## 4. Interpretation checks (do the conclusions follow?)

No mature validated end-to-end tool exists. The pieces that do:

### 4.1 Causal overclaiming: prevalence, rubrics, and the 2026 anchor result

- **Haber, N., Smith, E. R., Moscoe, E., Andrews, K., Audy, R., Bell, W., et al. (2018). *Causal language and strength of inference in academic and media articles shared in social media (CLAIMS): a systematic review*. PLOS ONE, 13(5): e0196346. doi:10.1371/journal.pone.0196346.** 50 most-shared academic articles of 2015 plus covering media. **34% of academic studies and 48% of media articles used language too strong for their strength of inference.** 70% of studies were low or very low strength of inference; only 6% high or very high. Supplies a validated two-axis rubric (strength of causal language × strength of causal inference) with human-rated reference data — directly implementable as an LLM rubric task with a known human baseline.
- **Haber, N., Wieten, S., Rohrer, J., Arah, O., Tennant, P., Stuart, E., et al. (2022). *Causal and associational language in observational health research: a systematic evaluation*. American Journal of Epidemiology, 191(12), 2084–2097. doi:10.1093/aje/kwac137.** 1,170 articles, 18 journals, 2010–2019. Causal implication of linking language: none 13.8%, weak 34.2%, moderate 33.2%, strong 18.7%. **Implied causality of action recommendations exceeded that of linking sentences in 44.5% of articles** — the recommendation is where the overclaim escalates. Most common linking word: "associate" (45.7%).
- **Isch, C., Dörr, T., Fasching, N., Jennings, G., & Watts, D. J. (2026). *Quantifying the prevalence and impact of overreaching causal claims in social science*. Nature Human Behaviour. doi:10.1038/s41562-026-02553-x (24 Aug 2026).** The most relevant paper in this section and it is a week old. LLM analysis of **194,631 cross-sectional articles**: **46%** contained causal language in titles/abstracts over 1980–2024, rising from **20% (2000) to 60%** — a near-threefold increase. Human-subjects experiment (N = 1,105): readers read causal phrasing as causal evidence; methodological labels (β = −0.4, 95% CI −0.56 to −0.19) and associational wording (β = −0.3, 95% CI −0.43 to −0.07) reduce this. **Five LLMs summarising these articles (N = 100 each) amplified causal overstatement — removing hedges and introducing causal claims where the article was strictly associational — though prompting for caution reduced it.**
- Adams, R. C., Challenger, A., Bratton, L., Boivin, J., Bott, L., Powell, G., et al. (2019). *Claims of causality in health news: a randomised trial*. BMC Medicine. doi:10.1186/s12916-019-1324-7

### 4.2 Causal-claim NLP classifiers — where the working tools are

- **Yu, B., Li, Y., & Wang, J. (2019). *Detecting causal language use in science findings*. EMNLP-IJCNLP 2019. doi:10.18653/v1/D19-1473.** >3,000 PubMed conclusion sentences; BERT classifier over four classes — *no relationship / correlational / conditional causal / direct causal*. **Accuracy 0.90, macro-F1 0.88.** Applied to ~38,000 observational studies: **21.7%** used direct causal language exclusively in conclusions, **32.4%** used some. Code: github.com/junwang4/causal-language-use-in-science. The strongest verified pre-LLM classifier for exactly this task.
- Follow-up: *Measuring correlation-to-causation exaggeration in press releases* (COLING 2020, same group). **22%** of press releases exaggerated correlational findings into causal claims; universities exaggerated more than journal publishers, 1.5:1.
- **Norouzi, R., Kleinberg, B., Vermunt, J. K., & van Lissa, C. J. (2025). *Capturing causal claims: a fine-tuned text mining model for extracting causal sentences from social science papers*. Research Synthesis Methods. doi:10.1017/rsm.2024.13.** 1,058 manually annotated sentences; BERT fine-tuned on social-science data: **F1-macro 0.89, precision 0.87, recall 0.91**. Domain-specific fine-tuning added 7–15% F1; general-purpose fine-tuning *lost* 4–18% on domain text. Code: github.com/rasoulnorouzi/cessc.
- **Saklad, R., Chadha, A., Pavlov, O. V., & Moraffah, R. (2026). *Can large language models infer causal relationships from real-world text?* ACL 2026.** First real-world benchmark for causal-relation extraction from academic literature. **Best model: average F1 0.535.** Benchmark: github.com/Ryan-Saklad/ReCITE. **Zero-shot LLMs are substantially worse at this than a fine-tuned domain classifier.**
- Wang, J., & Yu, B. (2025). *Causal language in observational studies: sociocultural backgrounds and team composition*. arXiv:2502.12159. >90,000 abstracts.
- **`metacheck::causal_claims`** already extracts causal-claim sentences and checks whether random assignment is reported — a working starting point inside the ecosystem.

### 4.3 Spin detection

- **Boutron, I., Dutton, S., Ravaud, P., & Altman, D. G. (2010). *Reporting and interpretation of randomized controlled trials with statistically nonsignificant results for primary outcomes*. JAMA, 303(20), 2058–2064. doi:10.1001/jama.2010.651.** The canonical spin taxonomy. 72 RCTs with nonsignificant primary outcomes; spin most common in abstracts, present in the main text of nearly 30%.
- Yavchitz, A., Boutron, I., Bafeta, A., et al. (2012). PLoS Medicine. doi:10.1371/journal.pmed.1001308. Boutron, I., Haneef, R., Yavchitz, A., et al. (2019). BMC Medicine. doi:10.1186/s12916-019-1330-9.
- **DeSpin** — Koroleva, A., Kamath, S., Bossuyt, P., & Paroubek, P. (2020). *DeSpin: a prototype system for detecting spin in biomedical publications*. BioNLP 2020, aclanthology.org/2020.bionlp-1.5/. First spin-detection tool; rule-based plus ML over text structure, sentence classification, entity/relation extraction and semantic similarity. **Component F-measures 79.42–97.86%** across subtasks (extracting reported outcomes is hardest). Code: github.com/aakorolyova/DeSpin.
- **Perlis, R. H. (2025). *Characterizing spin in psychiatric clinical research literature using large language models*. JAMA Network Open. doi:10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2024.59500.** GPT-4 Turbo over **663 abstracts** from AJP, Lancet Psychiatry and JAMA Psychiatry, 2013–2023. **296 (44.6%)** showed possible or probable spin (RCTs 43.5%, meta-analyses 49.3%). Validation on 60 gold-standard abstracts: **sensitivity 100% (95% CI 98–100), specificity 91% (86–95), overall accuracy 95.8%.** **The strongest verified accuracy figure for an LLM applied to a QRP-adjacent judgement anywhere in this scan** — and note what makes it work: a narrow, well-defined, single-construct judgement over a short text, with a gold standard.
- Koechli, C., Dennstädt, F., Schröder, C., Aebersold, D. M., Förster, R., Zwahlen, D. R., & Windisch, P. (2026). *Large language models for supporting clear writing and detecting spin in randomized controlled trials in oncology*. JMIR Cancer. doi:10.2196/78221. 250 two-arm single-endpoint RCTs, 7 journals, 2005–2023; GPT-3.5 Turbo, GPT-4o, GPT-o1 across four input conditions. **[unverified: the F1 range 0.932–0.98 for GPT-o1 comes from a search snippet; the study's existence and design are confirmed.]**
- **Yun, H. S., Zhang, K. Y. C., Kouzy, R., Marshall, I. J., Li, J. J., & Wallace, B. C. (2025). *Caught in the web of words: do LLMs fall for spin in medical literature?* arXiv:2502.07963; CHIL 2025.** 22 LLMs vs humans on Boutron-derived spin-containing abstracts. **LLMs are across the board more susceptible to spin than humans**, and propagate it into plain-language summaries — but *can* recognise it when prompted explicitly. Pairs directly with the Isch et al. finding that LLM summaries amplify causal overstatement.

### 4.4 Claim–evidence consistency benchmarks

- **CLAIMCHECK** (§2.3) — the closest to "does the critique attach to the right claim", and LLMs underperform experts on exactly that binding.
- **PRISMM-Bench** — Selch, L., Hou, Y., Mirza, M. J., Doveh, S., Glass, J., Feris, R., & Lin, W. (2025/26). arXiv:2510.16505; ICLR 2026. **384 real inconsistencies across 353 papers**, mined from actual reviewer comments (not synthetic errors) and human-verified. Tests detection and resolution of inconsistencies **across text, figures, tables and equations**. The "grounded in real reviewer-found inconsistencies" construction is the right template for evaluating a statistical-review pipeline.
- **PaperAsk** — Wu, Y., Liu, X., Feng, Y., Ding, J., & Ma, X. (2025/26). arXiv:2510.22242; WWW 2026, doi:10.1145/3774904.3792597. GPT-4o, GPT-5 and Gemini-2.5-Flash under realistic web-interface conditions. **Citation retrieval fails in 48–98% of multi-reference queries; section-specific content extraction fails in 72–91% of cases; topical paper discovery F1 < 0.32.**
- **DeepSciVerify** — Sadeghi, S., Khajavi, K., Adhikari, R., & Tessier, A. (2026). arXiv:2605.27710. Two-stage: verify from the abstract, escalate to full-text passages only for uncertain cases. Evaluated on the SCitance benchmark. A cost-control pattern worth copying.
- **AutoVerifier** — Du, Y., Dinh, M., Zhang, K., & Li, N. (2026). arXiv:2604.02617. Decomposes claims into (subject, predicate, object) triples over a six-layer knowledge graph. Demonstrated on a single case, **not benchmarked** — take the "overclaim escalation" pattern it names (hedges present in the methods, dropped in abstract and conclusion) as a check design, not as validated performance.

### 4.5 Explicit negatives — do not chase these

- **"Koroshetz"** does not exist in this literature; it is a garbling of **Koroleva** (DeSpin's first author).
- **There is no biomedical spin-detection tool called "SpinBot"** — spinbot.com is a commercial article-*spinning* (paraphrasing) service.
- **"Han et al." on causal language** did not resolve to any paper.
- **"TRUST"** did not resolve as a preregistration-checking tool. The nearest real things are **INSPECT-SR** (Wilkinson, …, Hussey, …, Kirkham, 2026, in press, BMJ; medRxiv doi:10.1101/2025.09.03.25334905) and an NLP tool generating CONSORT checklists (PMC7545295).
- **A circulating "GPT-4 detected ~53% of deliberately inserted errors, approaching human reviewer performance" figure** has no traceable source. Do not cite it.
- **A "12-pattern automated overclaim detection system"** attributed in marketing material (manusights.com) does not exist in the literature. The 194,631 / 46% figures belong to Isch et al. (2026) in Nature Human Behaviour.

## 5. Design implications

### 5.1 Wrap (mature, do not rebuild)

| Capability | Wrap what | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Paper parsing into a structured object | `metacheck` (Grobid → paper object: sentences, tables, references) | The biggest build-vs-wrap saving. Adopt the paper object as the pipeline's internal representation. Ingestion detail is thin in the docs — read the source |
| Test-statistic/p consistency | `statcheck`, or `metacheck::stat_check` | **Report coverage alongside flags.** A flag is ~60% likely to be a real inconsistency once unrecognised tests are counted |
| Statistics reported in **tables** | `tableParser` (Böschen 2026, arXiv:2603.19756) | statcheck's biggest blind spot; `checkPDF()` found 0 of 1,148 hand-coded results in one evaluation |
| Higher-recall extraction | `JATSdecoder` (Böschen 2021, doi:10.1038/s41598-021-98782-3) | 1,095 vs statcheck's 129 results on the same 49 articles. Use it as the extractor and statcheck's recomputation as the checker |
| Granularity consistency | `scrutiny` (GRIM, GRIMMER, DEBIT, duplication analysis, `audit()` layer), `rsprite2` (SPRITE), CORVIDS for exhaustive recovery | Cheap; run always where means/SDs of bounded-granularity items and exact N are reported. Note GRIM has no power once N ≥ 100 for 2-decimal means |
| Bundled error-detection suite | `ERROR` R package (github.com/ianhussey/ERROR) | statcheck + GRIM + GRIMMER + SPRITE + effect-size recalculation in one place |
| Reference integrity | `metacheck` `ref_*` (CrossRef, Retraction Watch, PubPeer, FLoRA) | Solved problem |
| Open-material discovery and download | `metacheck` `osf_*` / `github_*` / `zenodo_*` / `rbox_*` / `aspredicted_*` | Directly reusable as the data/code acquisition stage |
| Open-data / transparency detection with published accuracy | ODDPub (doi:10.5334/dsj-2020-042), rtransparent (doi:10.1371/journal.pbio.3001107) | The only tools in this space that publish sensitivity/specificity |
| Registration↔paper comparison | RegCheck (arXiv:2601.13330), or `metacheck`'s `reg_check` wrapper | No accuracy guarantees yet — the authors say so explicitly |
| Multiverse execution | `multiverse` (branch syntax) or `specr` (regression grids); `boba` for a visual explorer | Do not write a new grid runner |
| Multiverse inference | PIMA sign-flipping score test (doi:10.1007/s11336-024-09973-6) | A valid global test rather than a descriptive curve |
| Multiverse declaration format | SMART schema (doi:10.1098/rsos.250800) | Interchange format between a specification *generator* and the *executor* |
| Large-multiverse visualisation | Krähmer & Young multiverse plots (doi:10.1371/journal.pone.0339452) | Spec curves need distorting sampling above a few thousand specifications |
| Causal-sentence extraction | Norouzi et al. `cessc` (F1-macro 0.89) or Yu et al. (macro-F1 0.88); `metacheck::causal_claims` | **Fine-tuned domain classifiers beat zero-shot LLMs here (0.89 vs 0.535 F1).** Do not replace them with a prompt |
| Structured record of an analyst's own justification | Subjective Evidence Evaluation Survey, 18 items from a 37-expert Delphi (doi:10.1098/rsos.240125) | Makes an LLM analyst's stated reasoning comparable to the human many-analysts corpora |
| Pipeline regression tests | CORE-Bench (arXiv:2409.11363), BLADE (arXiv:2408.09667), SPOT (arXiv:2505.11855), PRISMM-Bench (arXiv:2510.16505) | Measure the pipeline before trusting it on new papers |
| Human-reanalyst comparison baselines | Multi100 (doi:10.1038/s41586-025-09844-9) and Holtdirk et al. (arXiv:2606.13670), both on ±0.05 Cohen's d | The only pair of studies with a shared tolerance — use them as the acceptance target |

### 5.2 Build (no mature tool exists)

1. **LLM specification-space generation with a principled/defensible filter.** Nothing generates a specification space from a paper's methods section. The gap is not generation but *screening*: specifications are not exchangeable (Del Giudice & Gangestad), and an AI auditor screen still leaves a persona-steerable distribution (Bertran et al., PNAS 2026). Build generator → auditor → **and a record of which specifications were rejected and why**, because the rejection rule is where the result is determined.
2. **An extremeness statistic for the reported analysis.** The m-value / Agentic Bootstrap (arXiv:2607.01507) is the right output shape: not "the paper is wrong" but "the paper's estimate sits at percentile X of its own defensible analysis space." Reimplement it and report the sampling procedure, since the estimate depends on it.
3. **Perturbation sanity checks on any agent-produced analysis.** Rewolinski et al. (arXiv:2604.11003): noise injection and reasonable perturbations, run on the pipeline's own output, because agent self-confidence is uncalibrated. Cheap; make it non-optional.
4. **Analytic-appropriateness judgement.** The least-evidenced capability in this scan: one 20-item vignette study at ceiling (Cureus 2025, 100%) and StatABench's 68.6% on closed-form statistics with methodological decision-making named as a failure mode. If the pipeline depends on it, you must generate the evidence — a seeded-error design over real manuscripts in the target domain (the Liu–Shah / FLAWS method) is the established way. Follow CLAIMCHECK: require the model to cite the specific claim and the specific result, and score the binding, not the prose.
5. **Interpretation/overclaiming check.** Implementable now from the Haber rubric (34% academic overclaim baseline; the AJE finding that *action recommendations* overclaim in 44.5% of articles) plus a hedge-drop comparison across sections. Must be built and validated. Perlis (2025) shows a narrow single-construct LLM judgement can reach 100/91 sensitivity/specificity against a gold standard — that is the shape to aim for, not open-ended critique.
6. **Multiverse-dimension bookkeeping when LLMs are themselves measurement instruments.** Linde et al. (arXiv:2605.19745): model choice and parse-failure rate are specification dimensions. If the pipeline uses an LLM to code variables, that model is a fork in the garden and must appear in the specification curve, with failure rate reported alongside the estimate.
7. **Reproduction from the methods description, not the authors' code.** Kohler et al. (arXiv:2604.21965) is the closest existing design: structured method extraction, information isolation, deterministic cell-level comparison against published tables, and error attribution separating agent error from paper underspecification. Adopt that architecture rather than inventing one.
8. **Environment reconstruction, before anything statistical.** 98.8% of R supplements on OSF lack executable dependency descriptions and only 25.87% run after automated Docker reconstruction (arXiv:2505.21590); 74% of 9,000+ R files from Harvard Dataverse failed to complete, 56% after automatic cleaning (doi:10.1038/s41597-022-01143-6). This is where a reproduction pipeline actually fails. Budget for it as a first-class stage with its own success metric, and reuse the containerisation approach rather than writing a new one. **Xu & Yang reach 87% end-to-end (100% conditional on accessible materials) precisely by making this the agent's whole job** while humans fix the diagnostic templates — versus ~21% for free-form agents asked to reason and execute at once.
9. **Pin the estimand before generating specifications.** Auspurg & Brüderl (doi:10.1177/23780231211024421) show most of Silberzahn's dispersion came from an unclear research question; Rohrer, Hullman & Gelman make the same point — a multiverse fails inferentially when its analyses "do not target a coherent estimand." The pipeline needs an explicit estimand statement as an *input*, not an inferred one.
10. **Automatic enumeration of decision points from existing analysis code.** This is the clearest unfilled gap in the whole tooling landscape. Every multiverse tool requires hand-annotated `branch()` markup or a hand-typed decision grid. Nothing reads a script and proposes its forking paths — and this is exactly what an LLM plausibly can do, since it is a code-comprehension task with a checkable output (the enumerated decisions can be validated against the code by re-execution). `rdfanalysis`'s auto-generated design flowcharts and the SMART schema give the output format.
11. **Instrument estimate and verdict separately.** Miao et al. find agents reproduce human dispersion in *estimates*; Alizadeh et al. find estimates stay stable while a confirmatory prompt swings the *verdict* from 10% to 90% support. They do not move together, so a single "did the agent agree?" metric hides the failure mode.

### 5.3 Design rules carried from the evidence

- **Targeted checks beat holistic review**, in every study that compared them (ReviewerGPT 2023, FLAWS 2025, Zhang & Abernethy 2026, PaperAudit-Bench 2026). Decompose into named checks; do not build a general critique agent.
- **Precision comes from scope restriction.** The one 83.2%-precision LLM result restricted itself to arithmetic, formulas, figures and tables. At SPOT's 6.1% you get ~15 false alarms per real error. YesNoError is the worked example of what publishing at that rate costs; Black Spatula's refusal to publish flags is the considered alternative; the 2016 statcheck/PubPeer backlash is the precedent.
- **The RegCheck pattern is the defensible architecture for judgement calls:** LLM extracts and aligns text with verbatim excerpts and relevance scores → deterministic checkers decide what can be decided exactly → the human makes the discrepancy call. This is what the people who run the human-expert error-detection programme actually built.
- **Adopt non-inferiority to human–human agreement as the evaluation target, or argue explicitly against it.** Ground truth is often unavailable — human coder concordance on the *number of hypotheses in a preregistration* is 14%.
- **N AI reviewers ≠ N opinions.** AI–AI overlap 21% vs human–human 3%. But multi-model *union* is the cheapest accuracy lever available (71.6% → 83.3% recall), and aggregating across runs matters because SPOT found errors were rarely rediscovered across eight independent runs. Budget for repeated runs.
- **Feed source, not PDF.** Up to a 13-point HR@5 swing between LaTeX and PDF input; statcheck's `checkPDF()` found zero results where `checkHTML()` found 129.
- **LLMs make causal overclaiming worse by default.** LLM summaries strip hedges and introduce causal claims (Isch et al., NHB 2026); LLMs are more spin-susceptible than humans (Yun et al., CHIL 2025). Both find explicit cautionary prompting reduces it. Any stage that summarises or paraphrases findings needs that guardrail *and* a check that the guardrail works. Never feed a paper's own abstract as context for judging that abstract.
- **Retrieval is the weak link.** 72–91% failure on section-specific content extraction (PaperAsk). Ground every extracted number in a located span in the parsed paper object; never let the model recall it.
- **Adversarial robustness is not optional.** Hidden-text injection moved acceptance from 0% to near 100% and specifically degraded methodology-flaw detection; tampering with 5% of reviews moved 12% of papers out of the top 30%.
- **Dispersion is not diagnosis.** Breznau et al. found analytic choices explained little variance; Mathur et al. (PNAS 2023) found 90% of Breznau's standardised estimates fell within ±0.037 — significance flips easily around a near-null estimate at large n. Report the distribution of *magnitudes*, not the proportion of significant specifications.
- **Benchmark against humans, not against the paper.** Human reanalysts recover the original effect size within ±0.05 d only **34%** of the time (Multi100, Nature 2026). An agent at 24–40% is in the human band. "Did not reproduce the published number" is not by itself evidence about the agent or about the paper.
- **Analytical heterogeneity dominates population heterogeneity** (Holzmeister et al., PNAS 2024) — and within analytical choices, **data cleaning is the largest single source** (Huntington-Klein et al. 2025: sample-size IQR collapsed from 295,187 to near zero once cleaned data were shared). Enumerate cleaning and inclusion decisions first; they matter more than model choice.
- **Dispersion is not universal.** fNIRS teams agreed ~80% of the time (Communications Biology 2025), response-time modelling teams converged (Dutilh et al. 2018), and >85% of economics/political-science papers reproduced computationally with 72% of significant estimates surviving robustness checks (Nature 2026). A pipeline that only ever reports instability is miscalibrated.
- **Only genuinely uncertain decisions belong in the multiverse.** Del Giudice & Gangestad's Type E / Type N / Type U taxonomy: including principled-non-equivalent alternatives buries a real effect in poorly justified noise. This is the screening rule the generator needs.
- **Do not weight specifications as a safety net.** Bayesian model averaging helps a well-specified multiverse but *amplifies* error when a collider is in the model space, and does not beat uniform weighting otherwise (Cantone & Tomaselli 2024).
- **Instability is not uncertainty.** Slez (2019): the modelling distribution is not a sampling distribution. If the pipeline reports an interval across specifications, say what it is an interval *of*. PIMA (doi:10.1007/s11336-024-09973-6) is the route to an actual inferential statement.
- **Report the worst case, and treat a large multiverse as a warning.** Modrák (2026)'s two conventions, offered against the use of multiverse analysis to manufacture doubt or to avoid committing to a conclusion.
- **AI review does not filter contradictory analyses.** 86% AI-review and 78% human-expert pass rates for mutually contradictory analyses (arXiv:2607.01507). A "reviewed by an LLM" stage adds little unless it is scored against a benchmark.
- **Measure the pipeline's own precision and recall and publish it.** ERROR's bottleneck is human reviewer supply, not error supply; the value of automation is throughput *at a stated error rate*. Every tool in this scan that publishes accuracy is more useful than every tool that does not.

## 6. Known gaps in this scan

- No published validation for any `metacheck` module, and no paper describing the package.
- RegCheck's underlying model and prompt pipeline are undisclosed ("Parity Engine"); no accuracy numbers exist yet.
- ERROR has published no aggregate error rate; completed-review counts differ between the project site (6) and *Science* reporting (9, one error-free).
- Python coverage of the GRIM/SPRITE family is essentially absent — only a GRIM package exists.
- Stata multiverse packages (`specurve`, `speccurve`) were not verified.
- Bauer & Francis on GRIM/GRIMMER could not be located; only their Expressions of Concern were found.
- CORVIDS, the statcheck validity study, and GRIMMER exist as preprints only.
- Auspurg & Brüderl's Breznau reanalysis (SocArXiv doi:10.31222/osf.io/uc84k) and the EEGManyPipelines results paper (MetArXiv doi:10.31222/osf.io/wvx48_v1) are preprints with no published version located. Same for Holtdirk et al. (arXiv:2606.13670), Rohrer/Hullman/Gelman (doi:10.31234/osf.io/37g29), Modrák (arXiv:2607.14623), and Huntington-Klein et al. 2025 (SSRN).
- **Holtdirk et al. — what was NOT verified:** which model(s) the pipeline used, the pipeline architecture, and the exact mechanics of the ±0.05 Cohen's d tolerance (absolute difference? how were non-d effects converted? how was the human-comparison subset selected and how large is it? are the LLM–human comparisons on matched studies?). **Treat the Multi100 head-to-head as indicative, not exact**, until the methods section is read.
- **"Aleph" as a reproducibility agent could not be verified.** No arXiv match; the Institute for Replication site lists only an unspecified "AI Engine." Likely referents: the COS/ReplicatorBench programme, REPRO-Bench, or the Xu & Yang workflow.
- A circulating "76% human / 86% agent" red-cards figure is **not** in Bertran et al. Do not reintroduce it.
- The three LLM output-homogeneity preprints cited as a caveat in §3.4 (arXiv:2604.18005, 2605.17193, 2601.06116) were not independently fetched.
- No published Simonsohn↔Del Giudice exchange was located; check datacolada.org before citing one.
- Breznau's reply to Mathur (PNAS e2219555120) was seen only as a cross-reference inside the Mathur letter, not resolved against Crossref.
- Milliways (CHI 2024): paper confirmed, no released software artefact located.
- Julia multiverse packages and Stata `speccheck`/`multishell`: negative findings from partial search, not proven absence.
- Two internal inconsistencies in source papers, noted but not resolved: **Breznau et al.** print "28.5% (23 of 89)" for teams concluding support, but 23/89 = 25.8%; **Gould et al.** report 141 blue-tit and 85 Eucalyptus usable effects in the abstract while the Zr meta-analysis tables use 131 and 79.
- The web-search budget for this session was exhausted partway through; the last stretch of verification ran on the Crossref, arXiv and OSF APIs plus direct page fetches. Coverage of very recent, non-indexed material (project websites, news reporting) is therefore thinner than the peer-reviewed and preprint coverage.
